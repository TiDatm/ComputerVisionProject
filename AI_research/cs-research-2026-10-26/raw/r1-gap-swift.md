## Summary

- **Definition.** The newest and best citable definition is Aljalbout et al. 2025 (arXiv 2510.20808, accepted to Annual Review of Control, Robotics, and Autonomous Systems 2026). In short: no simulator is perfect, so sim and reality always differ, and that difference is the "reality gap". The paper splits the sources into dynamics, perception and sensing, actuation and control, and system design (communication delays, latency). It also separates the *reality gap* (how much the worlds differ) from the *performance gap* (how much a given policy's score drops). The historical origin of the term is Jakobi, Husbands and Harvey (ECAL 1995).
- **Swift (Nature, 30 Aug 2023).**
  - Perception: a six-level U-Net segments gate corners in greyscale images from an Intel RealSense T265. The gate pose from the detector is fused with a visual-inertial estimate by a Kalman filter.
  - The policy never sees pixels. It sees a 15-D state estimate, the 4 corners of the next gate relative to the drone (12-D), and its previous action.
  - Closing the gap: there was **no** dynamics randomization. Instead, the team built residual models from **3 real rollouts (about 50 s of flight)**: a Gaussian process for perception errors and k-nearest-neighbour regression (k=5) for dynamics errors. They plugged these into the simulator and fine-tuned the policy once.
- **Swift results.** Swift won **15 of 25 races**: 5/9 against A. Vanover, 4/7 against T. Bitmatta and 6/9 against M. Schaepper. It also set the fastest race time, about 0.5 s ahead of Vanover's best. 40% of its 10 losses were collisions with the opponent. The paper states its own limits: it assumes the environment looks the same as in training (it is not robust to illumination changes) and it cannot recover after a crash. UZH's press release says it failed when the room was too bright.
- **Lineage, showing how the gap was closed step by step:**
  - 2019 (Loquercio, T-RO): a CNN trained only in randomized sim. It completed the track 95% of the time, but its best lap was 12.1 s against a professional pilot's 5.0 s.
  - 2020 (Kaufmann, RSS): acrobatics with 90-100% real success, using "feature tracks" as an abstraction instead of raw pixels.
  - 2021 (Loquercio, Science Robotics): forest flight at up to 40 km/h, trained only in simulation.
  - 2023: Swift beats the champions.
  - 2024 (Geles, RSS): pixels (a segmentation mask) to commands with no state estimation, 40 km/h, 100% over 20 real laps.
  - 2025 (TU Delft, A2RL Abu Dhabi, April): one rolling-shutter camera plus an IMU, beat 3 human world champions in an official knockout, up to 28 m/s (about 100 km/h).
  - 2025 (SkyDreamer): end-to-end from mask to motors, 21 m/s and 6 g, using **CycleGAN image translation** to make simulated masks look real.
- **Classic landmarks with numbers:**
  - Tobin 2017: an object detector trained only on random textures, 1.5 cm accuracy in reality.
  - CAD2RL 2017: a drone trained with no real images. It flew about twice as far between collisions as the baseline in real hallways.
  - Peng 2018: a real arm pushing task, 89% success with dynamics randomization against 0% without.
  - OpenAI 2019: a hand solved the Rubik's cube 60% / 20% of the time (by difficulty) after about 13,000 years of simulated experience.
  - Hwangbo 2019: ANYmal ran 25% faster than its previous record, using an actuator model learned from under 4 minutes of real data.
  - Miki 2022: a 2.2 km Alpine hike with zero falls, summit reached in 31 min against 35 min on the human signage. Its perception uses depth cameras or LiDAR, **not RGB**.
- **A clean story for block 4.** Every successful system did two things. It made the simulator closer to reality (residual models, calibration, image translation). It also made the robot less sensitive to whatever stays different (randomization, abstractions such as gate corners or masks instead of raw pixels). Swift did both, with a very small amount of real data. *(This is synthesis, my inference.)*
- **Caveats.**
  - Swift's fine-tuning data used a motion-capture system for ground truth, so the "little real data" still needed special lab equipment.
  - Most of the lineage comes from one lab (UZH RPG, with Intel/Koltun as co-authors), so it is not independent replication. TU Delft's A2RL and SkyDreamer results are the main independent line.

## Plain-language explanations (for first-year students)

- **Simulator.** A video game for robots: a program that imitates physics and cameras so a robot can practise millions of times without breaking anything. Swift practised about one month of flying in under an hour of computer time.
- **Sim-to-real gap (reality gap).** The game is never a perfect copy of the world, so a skill learned in the game can fail on the real robot. The gap is *how different the two worlds are*. The performance gap is *how much worse the robot does in reality than in the game*.
- **Kinds of gap.**
  - *Looks*: lighting, textures, glare, motion blur.
  - *Sensors*: noisy cameras, a camera mounted at a slightly different angle.
  - *Physics*: air drag, motor strength, battery sag.
  - *Timing*: the camera picture arrives late, and commands take time to reach the motors.

  A drone at 100 km/h moves almost 1 m in 33 ms, so a small delay matters *(arithmetic, my inference)*.
- **Domain randomization (one line; block 2 owns it).** Make the game deliberately messy and varied, with random colours, lights and weights, so reality looks like "just one more variation".
- **Residual model (Swift's trick).** Fly the real drone for a short while (Swift needed about 50 s), record where reality differs from the game, and teach a small model to predict that "leftover error". Add this correction back into the game, then let the drone practise a bit more in the corrected game.
- **Gaussian process / k-nearest neighbours.** Two simple ways to learn a correction from few examples. k-NN says "look up the 5 most similar moments we recorded and average their errors". A Gaussian process draws a smooth curve through the recorded errors and also says how unsure it is.
- **Gate detector (U-Net).** A neural network that colours in, pixel by pixel, where the gate corners are in the camera picture. The drone's "brain" then only gets the corner positions, not the full picture. That makes it less bothered by background differences between the game and reality.
- **VIO (visual-inertial odometry) + Kalman filter.** VIO is dead reckoning: it works out "where am I?" from how the camera view shifts plus the motion sensor (IMU), but it slowly drifts. The Kalman filter combines VIO with "I can see gate 3 right there", trusting each one as much as it deserves, to stay accurate.
- **Abstraction / intermediate representation.** Give the learning brain simplified inputs that look the same in the game and in reality: gate corners, segmentation masks, feature tracks. Deep Drone Acrobatics showed that image-based policies failed on new backgrounds while the feature-track policy kept 100% success.
- **Image translation (CycleGAN).** A network that learns to restyle pictures from "game style" to "real-camera style" without needing matched pairs. SkyDreamer used it to make simulated gate masks look like messy real ones.
- **Zero-shot transfer.** Trained only in simulation, it works on the real robot first time with no real practice, as in Loquercio 2019, CAD2RL and MonoRace's deployed policy.
